## A carrier is a copy of a binding

[Part 2](/programming/authority-enforcement.html) put an enforcer in the path of every effect, applying a decision made by the database of [Part 1](/programming/authorization-models.html). The two are rarely in the same place, so something has to bring the answer to the enforcer.

Every stage in the [series grid](/programming/authorization-series-intro.html#from-a-chain-to-a-grid) rests on something written earlier: a rule, an attribute, a grant. At request time the stage has two ways to get it. It can look the fact up where it was written, or the request can bring a copy along. That copy is a **carrier**. On the grid, the two are the rows on either side of the reference monitors: Carried above, Looked up below. The monitor meets the request first, accepts what it carries, and looks up only what is missing.

Logging in with OpenID Connect hands you two carriers at once, and they carry different things:

| Carrier | What it says | A copy of |
|---|---|---|
| `id_token` | "Alice authenticated at issuer I, for client C, at time T" | an identity binding |
| `access_token` | "the bearer may call API R with scope S" | a decision |

The first belongs to authenticate, and a later series on identity covers it. The second belongs to decide, and it is what this article is about.

Jerry Saltzer's [RFC 1498][rfc1498] was written about network names, but it gives the two questions to ask of any carrier. The first is *binding time*: when was the copy made, and how long does it stay true? The second follows from it: the scope of a binding is wherever copies of it live. A decision that lives only in the PDP can be changed in one place. A decision copied into every token the PDP ever issued can only be changed by reaching every copy. Revocation, caching, expiry and delegation are all answers to those two questions.

## How much of the decision travels

Every model in [Part 1](/programming/authorization-models.html) decides at the resource. The subject presents a credential and the system works out what it means. But the credential can carry very different amounts of already-decided authority:

| Carrier | Example | What the verifier still decides |
|---|---|---|
| identity | "This is Alice." | everything |
| attributes | "Alice is a manager in Finance." | everything, with better facts |
| scoped token | "The bearer may read the billing API." | most of it is already decided |
| narrow token | "The bearer may read invoice 4471, until 17:00, once." | nothing: verification is all that remains |

Some carrier always travels: the enforcer cannot learn who is asking unless the request tells it, in a header or through the channel it arrived on. That is the top row. Every row below it is a cache of a decision. Going down the table moves work from request time to issue time. This is the sense in which tokens "shift left": the hard policy evaluation happens once, when the credential is minted, and every use after that is a cheap validity check. Presigned S3 URLs, OAuth access tokens and Kubernetes service account tokens all work this way. The authorization server does the expensive relationship and attribute work; the resource server checks a signature.

The table does not say whether the credential is a bearer instrument or bound to a key the holder must prove it controls. That question is *custody*, and it is orthogonal: an mTLS-bound token and a string in an `Authorization` header can sit on the same row with completely different theft properties. A later series on applied cryptography covers it.

### Policy and mechanism

The same table has a second reading, and it is the one that made the whole thing click for me: each row moves the seam between policy and mechanism.

![Three ways to open a door: a physical key, where policy equals mechanism; a hotel card reader backed by an ACL; and a key ring of capabilities.](/assets/authorization/keys-vs-cards.png)

A physical key fuses them. The shape of the key is the policy, and the lock's pins are the mechanism. To change the policy, you change the lock. A card key with a central ACL separates them. The reader is the mechanism and verifies identity; the database is the policy and decides rights. You get dynamic updates, central revocation and audit logs, at the price of an online check. A token puts the policy back in the artifact, as claims, and reduces the mechanism to verifying a signature. It is a physical key that dissolves after an hour and only works during business hours: the bearer model's offline simplicity, with some of the database model's contextual control.

So policy/mechanism separation is not a binary but a question of **where the seam sits**: down in the lock, out in a database, or inside the credential. Each row of the table pushes it further into the credential.

## Where, and when

[Karp][from-abac-zbac-evolution] splits access control into four steps: identification, authentication, authorization and access decision. On Unix, identification is creating the account. Authentication lets a process prove it runs on your behalf. Putting an entry in an ACL is an act of authorization. Checking that ACL is the access decision.

Every system performs the same four steps. They differ in where and when each one happens, and Karp names the two families. In **autheNtication-Based Access Control**, NBAC, the service works out who you are and decides from that. The ugly capitalization makes a point: identity, roles and attributes are all this one architecture. In **authoriZation-Based Access Control**, ZBAC, the request presents an authorization instead, and the service only has to verify it.

Two questions about those steps get collapsed constantly:

- **Where** is the authorization decision made: in the resource's domain, or the subject's?
- **When** is it made: at request time, or settled in advance and carried along?

They are independent, and two systems prove it:

| | Where | When |
|---|---|---|
| presigned URL | resource owner | in advance |
| ZBAC token | subject's domain | in advance |

Same binding time, opposite jurisdiction. "Shift left" is a claim about **when**, and it gets read as a claim about **where**. Moving a decision earlier buys latency and offline operation. Moving it into the subject's domain buys cross-organizational scale. You can have either without the other, and most systems that claim the second have only bought the first.

Karp's own table puts both questions to every step:

| | NBAC: where | NBAC: when | ZBAC: where | ZBAC: when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Identification | user domain | before request | user domain | before request |
| Authentication | service domain | at request time | user domain | before request |
| Authorization | **service domain** | before request | **user domain** | before request |
| Access decision | service domain | at request time | service domain | at request time |

Authorization is the row that matters. Under NBAC it happens in the service's domain, so your identity must mean something there: federated identity, single sign-on, PKI rationalization, and agreement on the meaning of every role and attribute you present. Under ZBAC it happens in your own domain, before you ask, and the service verifies an authorization rather than resolving an identity.

The payoff is cross-domain:

> After all, if my company signs a contract with your company, you have no way of knowing which of the permissions granted to my company should be granted to me; only my company knows.

Under NBAC that policy has to be communicated and reconciled. Under ZBAC my company takes a token from yours and delegates me a subset. No federated identity, no shared lexicon of roles, and your company learns nothing about my company's internal structure.

The **when** question has a cost: a decision settled in advance can go stale. [Part 2](/programming/authority-enforcement.html#what-can-change-between-check-and-use) took apart which of `f`'s four arguments can move between the decision and the effect, and [Fresh or frozen](#fresh-or-frozen) takes apart the trade.

## Possession is authorization

Macaroons, JWTs and OAuth access tokens are **artifacts**: a previous authorization, serialized so a later request need not return to the decision-maker. "Cached decision" is close but not exact. A verifier still checks signature, issuer, audience and expiry, and a JWT often carries *claims* from which the resource server makes a fresh decision rather than a final allow.

[Macaroons][macaroons-cookies-with-contextual] go furthest. A holder can attenuate one by appending caveats, such as a repository, a method, a time window, a request budget or a required third-party discharge, without the root key and without going back to the issuer:

```text
may access GitHub
    only repository X
    only pull-request operations
    before 17:00
    for sandbox Y
```

The constraints travel with the token instead of living in some verifier's memory, and a stolen macaroon is worth only what its caveats already permitted.

Bearer instruments long predate computers:

- **Value.** Casino chips, gift cards, subway tokens, postage stamps, gold, money orders.
- **Access.** House keys, coat check tickets, locker keys.
- **Authority.** Signet rings and royal seals: pressing the seal was the proof.
- **Computing.** OAuth bearer tokens, API keys, session cookies, presigned S3 URLs, "anyone with this link can edit", SSH private keys, TOTP seeds.

Notice what happened to almost all of them. Cash has serial numbers. Gift cards have activation systems. Crypto has a public ledger. Share links grow expiry dates and access logs.

That drift has a mechanism, and [Part 4](/programming/capabilities.html#keys-and-object-capabilities) names it precisely. A bearer token cannot be wrapped in a forwarder you could later sever, so revocation has to come from a central table that says which tokens are still good. And nothing bounds where a token goes, so finding out requires logging every use. Registries creep into bearer systems because a registry is the cheapest substitute for composability and confinement. Ptacek's [survey of API token formats][api-tokens-tedious-survey], from random strings to JWT, PASETO, macaroons and Biscuits, reaches the same place from the engineering side: the more a token carries, the harder it is to take back, and for most systems a random token looked up in a database is the right answer.

![Identity-based and bearer authorization each patch their weakness by importing the other's core mechanism: identity-based systems carry caller context against the confused deputy, bearer systems add a registry for revocation and audit, and both land on identity at issuance with a scoped carrier at use. Object capabilities are not on the picture.](/assets/authorization/identity-vs-bearer.svg)

The useful question is never whether something is bearer or registered. It is where on that spectrum it sits, and what it gave up to get there.

## Fresh or frozen

Every carrier makes the same trade. Look the decision up on every request, and it is as current as the store you read, but every request pays for the lookup and depends on whoever holds the facts. A replica that has not yet seen the revoking write is stale too, and [Part 1](/programming/authorization-models.html#freshness) shows how Zanzibar rules that out. Carry a copy, and the request works without a lookup, but the copy is frozen. A frozen decision cannot be told apart from a stale one once the facts it was made from change.

| | Freshness | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| re-evaluate | as current as the store | a lookup on every request |
| materialize | frozen at issue time | revocation debt |

The debt has three standard payments, and every system in this article uses at least one:

- **Short lifetimes.** The copy expires before the facts are likely to change. OAuth access tokens and Kubernetes service account tokens live for minutes or hours. A presigned S3 URL lasts at most a week.
- **Indirection you can sever.** The carrier points at something the issuer can invalidate, instead of holding the authority itself. A random token looked up in a database is the simplest form.
- **A revocation channel.** Whoever holds the facts tells whoever holds the copies when they change. [Flask](/programming/authority-enforcement.html#decide-vs-enforce) built this in 1999: its object managers cache access vectors, and the security server notifies them when a policy change revokes one.

None of the three is free. Short lifetimes put the issuer on the critical path of every renewal. Indirection brings the registry back. A revocation channel needs every holder of a copy to be reachable. [Part 2](/programming/authority-enforcement.html#what-can-change-between-check-and-use) followed the same trade into the races between a check and its use, where a frozen copy is exactly what closes them.

## Three kinds of delegation

Three different things get called delegation, and keeping them apart matters for the rest of the series:

- **Administrative delegation.** Alice gains the power to grant others access to X. She is not exercising authority over X; she is exercising authority over the *policy* about X. This is what "admin" usually means.
- **On-behalf-of delegation.** Bob acts *as* Alice. Impersonation, `sudo -u`, OAuth's actor claim, service accounts that assume a user's identity. Bob's effective authority is Alice's entire authority.
- **Authority delegation.** Alice gives Bob a specific power she holds, and only that. Bob acts as himself, holding one more thing than he did before.

On the grid, the three land in three different cells. Administrative delegation writes the rules that decide consults. On-behalf-of delegation hands over the subject, so authenticate sees Alice whoever is asking. Only authority delegation is acquire: Bob comes to hold one more name.

Only the last two hand over a carrier. On-behalf-of delegation hands over a copy of the subject. Authority delegation hands over a copy of one decision.

### The OAuth arc

The clearest evidence of which kind won is OAuth, the protocol most delegation on the internet runs through. It exists to answer one question: how do you let a third party act on your behalf *without* handing over your password? Four versions have answered it:

| Version | What changed |
|---|---|
| OAuth 1.0 / 1.0a (2007, RFC 5849) | Every request signed by the client with a shared secret, so security did not depend on the transport. Signature canonicalization was where every implementation broke. |
| [OAuth 2.0][oauth-2-0-authorization] (2012, RFC 6749 and 6750) | Bearer tokens over TLS, and a framework rather than a protocol. Its lead editor [resigned over it][oauth-2-0-road], and a decade of patches followed: PKCE, device flow, introspection, mTLS, DPoP and the [Security BCP][best-current-practice-oauth]. |
| OAuth 2.1 (draft) | Folds those patches into the baseline: PKCE required, the implicit and password grants removed, no bearer tokens in query strings, exact redirect matching. |
| [GNAP][grant-negotiation-authorization-protocol] (2024, RFC 9635) | A clean-slate redesign: no pre-registered clients, and key-bound tokens by default. Finished, but little adopted, because it breaks compatibility with OAuth 2. |

The movement is from bearer tokens toward key-bound ones. That is real progress on custody: how a credential is held.

It is progress on nothing else. Four redesigns never changed what the third party does: it still acts as you, with whatever the scope string happens to cover, and no version lets you hand over one specific power and only that. A scope is a coarse label on an impersonation, not an attenuation of an authority: on-behalf-of delegation wearing authority delegation's clothes. Closing that gap needs more than a delegation protocol. The delegate has to acquire a name that designates, reaches and decides one object and nothing else. OAuth only ever touched decide. The URL stays global and the network stays open, which is why no version of it could have closed the gap.

## Local authority versus global knowledge

Lampson states the administrative case bluntly in [a 2020 retrospective][authorization-lampson]:

> Only ACLs work for managing the policy, because the manager's question is, "Who has access to this resource?" It's okay to make short-term copies of parts of it into capabilities (usually called file descriptors), which are faster to check.

Hence the slogan: capabilities are cached ACL decisions. It is right about one thing and wrong about another.

It is right that a capability answers only one question, perfectly and locally: *may the holder of this perform operation X?* A central store answers the questions a capability cannot: who can perform X, what can Alice access, revoke everything derived from Alice's grant, and which of these ten thousand records may Alice see. That last one deserves its own name. **Reverse indexability**, filtering a result set by authorization rather than checking one access, is why systems like Zanzibar exist. You cannot build a list page by asking the user to present every capability they might hold.

It is wrong that capabilities have no administrative story. Karp's architecture has one, and it is centralized: a local domain authority holds the delegable authority its organization received, decides which users get which subsets, mints tokens and revokes them. That is a store, an admin console and a lifecycle.

> **Wherever authority is minted, someone keeps a store. The question is not whether there is a store, but how many there are and whose jurisdiction each sits in.**

ACLs put one store at the resource. ZBAC puts a store in each issuing domain. Object capabilities put one at every issuer, including every object that hands out a proxy. What capabilities genuinely lack is a *global* view, which [Part 4](/programming/capabilities.html#review-is-the-real-trade) reaches from the other direction.

And sometimes nothing is cached at all. In seL4, the capability graph is not a copy of some authoritative ACL kept elsewhere. There is no elsewhere: the graph *is* the authority, and there is no original to cache.

The two also govern different principals. Zanzibar's are people and organizational relations: they change independently of the code, non-programmers edit them, and they must be enumerable and revocable on demand. Capabilities govern code objects, where the adversary is a buggy or hostile component rather than an over-privileged employee. So running both is normal. Centralized policy decides what authority should exist, at the user-facing boundary. Capabilities represent and propagate that authority once it exists, below it. The interesting engineering is in the seam between them.

### What running both looks like

Take `GET /docs/4471`:

![The edge service resolves Alice's session and asks the relationship store check(user:alice, viewer, doc:4471). On allow, it mints a handle for doc 4471 with read, and hands it to the renderer and the export worker. The renderer wraps its handle into read for 30 seconds for the thumbnailer. All three reach the document store only through a handle; there is no superuser connection.](/assets/authorization/policy-then-handles.svg)

The renderer, the thumbnailer and the export worker have no database credentials and no way to name document 4472. The thumbnailer's copy is narrowed further by wrapping a reference that already exists, with no second trip to the policy store.

The missing superuser connection carries the whole design. If the renderer could fetch the row over the service's own connection, the check would be advisory, and every code path that skips it would reach everything. The capability layer is not an optimization of the ACL check. It is what closes the gap between the intended state and the reachable state. Revocation across the seam uses the same payments as any carrier: short lifetimes, indirection you can sever, or a channel back from the policy store.

Capsicum is this architecture compressed into one process: open what policy permits by path, then call `cap_enter()`, after which the process can never name anything new. Presigned URLs are the same architecture stretched across a network, where IAM decides at signing time and the holder never touches IAM.

## From one stage to several

Real systems mix these pieces rather than choosing one. Laid out in grid terms, the systems people compare most often cover different parts of it:

| System | Who writes the grant | What travels | Logic inside the carrier | Names and reach |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| OAuth 2 / OIDC | an authorization server, after the user consents | an access token; the `id_token` carries identity | scopes | URLs stay global; the network stays open |
| GNAP | an authorization server, by negotiation | access tokens, key-bound by default | the access rights requested | HTTP or other |
| SPKI / SDSI | the key holder, as a signed certificate it may delegate | a certificate chain | tags, intersected along the chain | out of scope |
| Macaroons | the issuer, then any holder, by adding caveats | the macaroon | caveats, including third-party ones | out of scope |
| Biscuit | the issuer, then any holder, by appending blocks | the token | Datalog facts, rules and checks | out of scope |
| Cap'n Proto | whoever passes the reference | an index into the peer's table; the authority stays there | none needed | the reference is the route |

Zanzibar would have no row: it is a decision engine with nothing to say about carriers. Cap'n Proto is the odd one out, because it has no carrier at all.

Lay the options out by how much they tie together, and they form a line:

![Five steps from bearer tokens to object capabilities, with single-stage carriers covered in Part 3, capabilities in Part 4, and custody in the applied crypto series](/assets/authorization/carrier-continuum.svg)

The first step on that line, from bearer to key-bound, is custody, which is cryptography's subject. The middle is this article: how much of the decision rides along, and who can take it back. The last two steps leave carriers behind. The holder presents a handle into a table someone else owns, a gateway or a runtime, not a copy of the authority. Every carrier before them travels apart from the name it is for. A token says what the bearer may do, and the URL still says where. [Part 4](/programming/capabilities.html) is about the artifact that closes that gap: one object that designates, reaches and decides at once.
