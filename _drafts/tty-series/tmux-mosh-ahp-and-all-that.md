# Raw mode, all the way up

TODO: merge https://hackmd.io/T4Tbo_74TvKIVVPKX27wtQ with this article.

The [last post](/programming/your-terminal-has-been-editing-everything-you-type.html) ended on a line I want to take further:

> Every flag the puzzles switch off is the kernel getting out of the way of somebody else's protocol.

That's what raw mode is. A program with its own protocol asks the layer in the middle to stop mangling it. This post is about what happens when you make that same request three more times, going up instead of down — and about the layer that finally can't get out of the way, because getting out of the way is the only thing it does.

## The map

Almost everything people stack on top of a terminal is solving exactly one of these, at exactly one point in the pipeline. The confusion in this space comes from treating them as competitors when most of them are neighbours.

| Concern | Solved by | Where it sits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Process survives client death | tmux, screen, dtach, AHP host | server-side session ownership |
| Connection survives network change | mosh, Eternal Terminal, autossh | transport |
| Local responsiveness under latency | mosh prediction, optimistic writes | client-side model |
| Many workspaces on one connection | tmux windows/panes, ssh `ControlMaster` | spatial multiplexing |
| Concurrent writers arbitrated | AHP sequencing, OT, CRDTs | write path |
| One state, N different UIs | AHP, any state-first protocol | render placement |
| Addressable, lazily-fetched history | AHP content-by-reference | storage model |
| Offline writes | Replicache, Automerge, Linear | client persistence |
| Auth and encryption | ssh, dev tunnels | transport |

Two things fall out of it. **Persistence and roaming are different axes** — tmux survives your laptop dying, mosh survives your IP address changing — which is why `mosh host -- tmux attach` is stacking two rows rather than being redundant. And **the stacking rule is positional**: two layers compose when they occupy different points in the pipeline and conflict when they want the same one. tmux and screen don't stack, because both want to own the session. tmux and AHP don't stack, because both want to be the fan-out point.

That last collision is the subject of this post. The rest is the long way round to it.

## Count the ttys

Run tmux over ssh. Between your keyboard and your shell there are now three ptys, not one:

![three stacked ptys between a laptop and a tmux pane: the laptop pty held by Ghostty and ssh, the host pty held by sshd and the tmux client, both raw, and the pane pty held by the tmux server and bash, the only one cooked, with echo returning up the whole chain](/assets/raw-mode-all-the-way-up/three-ptys.svg)

1. **On your laptop** — Ghostty on the master, `ssh` on the slave. `ssh` raws it the moment it allocates a remote pty.
2. **On the host** — `sshd` on the master, the tmux *client* on the slave. The tmux **server** raws this one, in `tty_start_tty()`, via `tcsetattr` on a file descriptor it received over a unix socket. The client `dup()`s its stdin and stdout and hands them over with `SCM_RIGHTS` during the handshake. So the process configuring your terminal is not the process attached to it.
3. **On the host, one level in** — the tmux server on the master, `bash` on the slave. Cooked. Default termios.

Only the third one does anything. When you run `stty -a` inside tmux you are querying that third pty, and `stty -echo` there changes what *every* attached client sees, because there is one struct and it lives on the server.

Which resolves a question the last post left open. The echo you see when you type is generated by the pane's line discipline, sent out the master, and redrawn on every client. Nobody echoes locally. That's the whole mirror-session effect, and it's also why `^C` reaches the right process group: the two outer disciplines have `ISIG` cleared, so `0x03` travels as an ordinary byte until it reaches the one discipline that still turns it into a signal.

Three ptys. Three `c_cflag`s reporting a baud rate. Zero wires.

## tmux is the emulator with the display amputated

The last post split a terminal emulator in half: an encoder turning key events into bytes, and a parser plus screen model turning bytes back into glyphs.

tmux implements the parser and the screen model. It does not implement the glyphs. `input.c` is a full ECMA-48 state machine; `grid.c` stores cells, not pixels. There is no font, no rasteriser, no framebuffer anywhere in the server.

So what goes back to your laptop is **escape sequences again** — re-serialised from the grid, filtered through that client's terminfo, sent as a damage diff against what that client was last known to have seen. Ghostty parses them a second time and draws. Two ECMA-48 parsers in series; only the outer one ever touches a pixel.

That amputation is exactly what makes multiplexing possible. A grid is cheap to fan out. A framebuffer isn't.

## The limit

Here's what the model can't do, stated as precisely as I can manage.

**There is one grid per pane**, sized from `struct window`. What tmux keeps per client is a view apparatus — terminfo capabilities, a cursor and attribute cache for diffing, a viewport offset, a status line. Not a second grid.

Follow the size down and it terminates at one `ioctl(TIOCGWINSZ)` on the pane's pty. **The application gets exactly one answer.** It lays out for that number and emits one stream of cells. There is no mechanism by which it could produce two layouts, because there is nothing to ask it twice.

Everything the `window-size` option does is choose who eats the mismatch. `smallest` gives big clients dead margin. `largest` gives small clients a crop they have to scroll — and the existence of `refresh-client -U/-D/-L/-R` is tmux admitting, in the API, that the content cannot reflow. `latest` resizes the window to whoever attached most recently, which means someone joining from a phone makes your 200-column layout collapse.

And the input direction is worse. tmux tracks which client a keystroke came from — it needs that for prefix bindings — but the moment the byte is written to the pane's pty master, the identity is gone. **There is no `client` field on a byte.** If two people are attached and both press `y` at a confirmation prompt, the program sees two `y` presses, indistinguishable from one person pressing `y` twice. That one isn't tmux's fault or the application's. It's the pty API.

There's a third limit worth naming because it turns out to be optional. The tmux client holds no model at all — every keystroke round-trips to the host before you see it, since the echo is generated by a line discipline three ptys away. That one is *not* forced by the grid. `mosh` keeps a screen model on both ends, syncs state diffs over UDP, and predicts your local echo, underlining the speculative characters until the server confirms. Optimistic writes with reconciliation, applied to a character grid. It just can't do tmux's other job: no sessions, no panes, no surviving a detach. Mosh solves latency, tmux solves persistence, and neither solves concurrent writers — which is why people run both, and why neither is what you reach for when two people want to drive the same session at once.

## Move the render, not the multiplexer

None of this is a defect in the TUI. It's the type signature of one. A terminal program's output is a stream of cells for a grid of known width; `vim` and `htop` are in exactly the same position.

The rule is: **whoever renders first destroys everyone downstream's ability to render differently.** tmux renders nothing of its own — it inherits cells and can only forward them. The commitment happened one box earlier.

So the fix isn't to make TUIs multi-client aware. It's to demote the TUI to one renderer among several and put the fan-out *above* it. Ship state; let each client lay itself out. That's [AHP](https://microsoft.github.io/agent-host-protocol/): a host owning an authoritative state tree, stamping mutations with a monotonic sequence, broadcasting to N subscribed clients, each rendering natively. Detach and reattach fall out for free — sessions keep running when no client is connected — but now a phone client can be a phone client instead of a cropped 200-column view.

## The punchline

Raw mode is the kernel getting out of the way of somebody else's protocol.

This is the *terminal* getting out of the way of somebody else's protocol. Same request, three layers up. Stop being a rendering pipeline, be a byte pipe, so the application's own protocol can reach a client that understands it.

And then the good part. AHP does not get rid of the terminal — it keeps ptys around, because agents run shell commands and shell commands want a controlling terminal. [Terminals](https://microsoft.github.io/agent-host-protocol/guide/terminals.html) get their own channel URIs, their own state, their own ownership claims. But read that page closely and you find the one place AHP switches its own machinery off:

> standard write-ahead reconciliation is not safe for terminals ... A pty is a stateful, mutable process — optimistically applying input or predicting output would produce incorrect state.

Every other resource in AHP is optimistic-with-reconciliation. Terminal input is side-effect-only; terminal output is server-authoritative and never predicted. Which is to say: a 2026 state-sync protocol, designing around the pty, arrives at exactly tmux's model — and fences it off as the special case where the modern approach doesn't apply.

The terminal doesn't go away. It gets demoted from the thing you look at to the thing you run commands in. Cooked mode survives at the bottom, one pty deep, doing echo and `icanon` and `icrnl` for a shell that no human is watching directly.

Someone is still rewriting the byte in flight. It's just further away now.
